## Evidence map

### Peru institutions and implementation

The strongest Peru-specific foundations are the CVR report, Laplante and
Theidon, and Odier Contreras-Garduño. Together they support the institutional
history of the CVR/PIR/PRC, victims' expectations of redress, the distinction
between recognition and ordinary development, implementation delays, project
caps, participation problems, and the risk that basic-service projects lose
their reparative meaning.

These sources do not establish the exact victimization-index formula, annual
CMAN treatment records, the realized priority rule, or the causal effect of
collective reparations. Those facts must be established from official
administrative sources and the project's data audit.

### Reparations, recognition, and transformative justice

De Greiff and Duthie, Gready and Robins, Moffett, Dixon, Rosenfeld, Teitel,
Elster, Falk, Kritz, and the United Nations principles provide the legal and
theoretical architecture. Important concepts include recognition of victims
as rights holders, corrective versus distributive justice, civic trust,
collective harm, symbolic and material reparation, guarantees of
non-repetition, resource constraints, and the distinction between transitional
and transformative justice.

This literature supports concepts and mechanisms. It generally does not
provide causal estimates of welfare, migration, or political behavior.

### Implementation, participation, and local capture

Firchow's Colombia work, Dixon, Justice Mosaics, Jeffery, and the Peru case
studies show that context, state capacity, local power asymmetry, technical
support, participation, and project selection can determine whether a program
is experienced as reparation, assistance, or ordinary public provision.

These findings motivate tests for heterogeneous implementation and political
economy. They do not prove that APRA alignment or any other Peruvian political
variable affected program assignment.

### Migration and place-based investment

Adhikari and Gentilini, Bastagli et al., Selod and Shilpi, Morley, and Lagakos
et al. support two competing mechanisms:

- **place-based anchoring:** local productive investment, services, risk
  reduction, or recognition can make remaining in a community more attractive;
- **mobility facilitation:** liquidity, human capital, networks, or reduced
  risk can make migration feasible, particularly for younger or more educated
  residents.

The direction of the effect is therefore an empirical question. Peru-specific
migration evidence in the notebook is mainly descriptive, and the project must
avoid attributing migration changes to reparations without a valid design.

### Comparative causal evidence

Guarin et al. provide a Colombian benchmark for the socioeconomic effects of
large individual cash reparations. Voytas studies political participation
after reparations in Chile. Villamil and Balcells provide evidence that a
symbolic transitional-justice policy can generate backlash in Spain.

These studies demonstrate plausible effect families and research designs.
Their treatments, institutions, units, and estimands differ materially from
Peru's community-level project program; their coefficients cannot be
transferred to this setting.

### Victim attitudes and political feedback

David and Choi provide correlational evidence on compensation, recognition,
forgiveness, and retributive preferences among Czech former political
prisoners. Voytas and Villamil and Balcells offer causal comparative evidence
on political participation and backlash.

For Victimas RD, recognition, trust, participation, and backlash remain
theoretically grounded outcomes or mechanisms. APRA alignment is a testable
pre-treatment allocation hypothesis, not an established fact and not an
instrument without separate justification.

## Identification and interpretation safeguards

1. The expanded notebook contains relevant modern RD-methods sources, but use
   it only for discovery and synthesis. Justify specifications from the
   verified underlying papers, official software documentation, and the
   project's empirical design evidence.
2. The substantive literature cannot establish that the administrative
   victimization thresholds caused a discontinuous change in treatment. That
   first stage must be demonstrated in the data within a predeclared risk set.
3. Treatment is assigned at the CCPP level, while migration and many mechanisms
   are individual or household outcomes. Preserve this level distinction and
   avoid ecological claims.
4. Post-treatment project type, trust, local management, services, or internet
   access are potential mechanisms or outcomes, not ordinary controls.
5. A conventional causal mediation analysis is not identified merely because
   treatment, mediator, and outcome are observed. Timing, treatment-induced
   confounding, and mediator identification require a separate design.
6. Later treatment of nominal controls must be aligned with each outcome date.
   Do not define the estimand by whichever treatment-year variable yields the
   strongest first stage.
7. Comparative evidence should be labeled by country, treatment type, unit,
   and evidence design whenever it is used to interpret Peru.

## Citation verification gate

No NotebookLM-derived claim may enter a manuscript or publication-facing table
note until all of the following are recorded:

1. the exact underlying source has been identified, with duplicates reconciled;
2. author, year, title, outlet or publisher, DOI or stable URL, and edition or
   version have been verified from the source or an authoritative index;
3. the supporting passage, table, or result has been read in the PDF and its
   page or locator recorded;
4. the evidence type has been labeled as legal, theoretical, qualitative,
   descriptive, correlational, quasi-experimental, or experimental;
5. the proposed sentence does not claim more than the source design supports;
6. Peru-specific, comparative, and general-theory evidence are distinguished;
7. a canonical BibTeX key has been checked against the live Overleaf
   bibliography; and
8. contradictory evidence and material limitations have been considered.

NotebookLM output should be paraphrased only after this gate. Never cite
NotebookLM, a chat answer, an upload number, or a truncated filename as the
scholarly source.

## Recommended query pattern

Use one NotebookLM session per coherent task and ask in passes:

1. inventory relevant sources and label evidence types;
2. extract convergent and conflicting claims;
3. request limitations and overreach risks;
4. separate Peru-specific evidence from comparative evidence and theory;
5. identify missing sources or official documents;
6. verify each proposed manuscript claim against the underlying files.

Keep requests small when producing source cards. Batches of about five sources
were materially more reliable than a single request for the entire notebook.
Reset or start a new session when the research question changes.
